What a 1st lien HELOC actually is

A 1st lien HELOC (also sold as an all-in-one loan or offset mortgage) replaces your mortgage entirely with a large line of credit secured by your home. It works in three moves:

  1. Draw once to pay off the mortgage. Day one, you borrow your full mortgage balance from the line. The mortgage is gone; the line is now the loan.
  2. Every paycheck goes into the line. Your deposit lands against the balance immediately, so you owe less starting that day.
  3. You live off the line. Bills and spending are drawn back out during the month. Interest is charged on the average daily balance, so money you haven't spent yet is quietly cutting your interest while it sits there.

The catch: the rate is variable and usually higher than a fixed mortgage, and there are two very different reasons the balance falls fast. Most of the speed comes from the surplus money you're putting in — which would pay down a regular mortgage just as fast. Only the leftover "float" advantage is unique to the HELOC. Before you sign anything, ask the lender

  • What index and margin set my rate, and what's the lifetime cap? "Prime + 1.5%, capped at 18%" tells you the worst case. Run that worst case above.
  • How is interest calculated? True average daily balance is the point of the product. If it's charged monthly on a period-end balance, the sweep advantage largely evaporates.
  • Can the lender freeze or reduce the line? Lines were frozen at scale in 2008. If your line is frozen, the money you swept in is no longer available to live on.
  • What exactly happens when the draw period ends? Get the required payment on the balance you'd realistically still owe.
  • What are all the fees? Closing costs, annual fee, early-closure fee, transaction or draw fees.

Who this tends to fit

The strategy rewards a household with steady income well above its spending, solid cash reserves, discipline about not treating home equity as spending money, and enough equity that the line comfortably covers the mortgage. If your surplus is thin or your income is lumpy, a variable rate against your house is a heavy bet — and the same surplus sent to your existing mortgage captures most of the benefit with none of the risk.
